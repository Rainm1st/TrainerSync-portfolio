Project Overview

Project Architecture

TrainerSync is a shared web application: adaptive workout cards + voice-first guidance + lightweight gym connect in one flow—stackable session planning, hands-free coaching cues, and on-floor help without a heavy social product.

Why this architecture matters

The system is not only a workout library. It connects exercise discovery, live physiological feedback, location-based social interaction, and coach oversight into one continuous training flow.

Hosted system link

The high-fidelity web prototype is available at https://rainm1st.github.io/CPT208-TrainerSync/.

1. Motivation & Research

The Why

Authorship — non-substantive AI per CPT208: All motivation, gap analysis, research synthesis, requirements, ideation, evaluation, and reflections are original Team C3-6 work. Illustrations may use generative tools only as visuals and never replace our research logic. Vibe coding disclosure: Technical Implementation → System build: vibe coding. AI-assisted outputs were reviewed for fit with the brief and for safe, non-clinical wording around vitals and coaching.

Our group chose the Active Lifestyles track because gym training is a highly social, physically demanding, and feedback-sensitive activity, yet many existing digital tools only support the workout before it starts or after it ends. In real gym sessions, coaches often need to supervise multiple trainees at once, while trainees need quick reassurance that they are using the right equipment, moving at the right pace, and staying within a safe and effective training state. We saw a clear opportunity to design something more human-centered than a static fitness log or video library. TrainerSync was therefore conceived as a connected training experience that links the coach dashboard and trainee interface through real-time status cues, lightweight telemetry logic, and shared session feedback. We were also interested in the playful dimension of active systems: rather than only recording what happened, the system should shape motivation in the moment. This track fits our strengths because it allowed us to combine interface design, interaction flow, fitness-domain content, and service thinking into one coherent project. In short, we selected Active Lifestyles because it offers a meaningful design challenge where better interaction design can directly improve confidence, safety, coordination, and sustained engagement.

  • Highlights hedonic motivation and gamification as practical satisfaction drivers.
  • Focuses on intention-to-use surveys more than real gym-floor behaviour.

  • Provides limited guidance on spatial equipment discovery in busy gym contexts.

  • Uses rigorous RCT-based meta-analysis rather than anecdotal app reports.
  • Shows that gamification can improve physical activity outcomes versus inactive controls.
  • Reports post-intervention durability instead of only short-term novelty effects.
  • Aggregates highly heterogeneous app designs, reducing direct design transferability.
  • Offers little detail on coach-facing supervision workflows.
  • Does not address multi-user coordination and messaging during live sessions.
Mair et al. (2023)
  • Provides an umbrella-level synthesis across many digital health intervention reviews.
  • Links concrete behaviour-change techniques to evidence (feedback, goals, social support).
  • Helps justify structured motivational features in a human-centred system.
  • NCD prevention framing dominates, while gym-specific use is secondary.
  • Gives limited UI-level guidance for trainee-coach interfaces.
  • Under-specifies playful micro-interactions at high-exertion moments.
Nishi et al. (2024)
  • Offers large pooled evidence on digital health app effectiveness.
  • Shows stronger effects when gamification elements are present.
  • Connects app usage to activity and cardiometabolic risk-related outcomes.
  • Primarily outcome-focused rather than interaction-design-focused.
  • Does not prescribe UX patterns for trainee-coach binding and monitoring.
  • Provides limited ethical interface guidance for real-time physiological displays.

Current Functional Requirements

Functional requirements = contractual “must do” behaviours. They outrank playful styling: the next subsection only shapes how those behaviours feel in use.

FR1 · Adaptive workout task cards

Objective: From target body areas, available equipment, session duration, and training level, the system assembles stackable multi-area task cards the trainee can combine for “today’s stack.” Each movement card carries a standard demo, a common-mistake contrast, substitute movements, and a short “why this today” intro so users quickly decide what to train and how to execute it correctly.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • Inputs for body focus, equipment on hand, time budget, and difficulty persist across the generator; changing one dimension updates the proposed stack without a full page reload in the HTML prototype.
  • Generated cards can stack or reorder; the UI shows how each card contributes to the session plan and warns before unsafe overload (e.g. duplicated maximal patterns).
  • Every prescribed movement exposes demo media, a “common errors” contrast panel, at least one substitute, and a one-line training rationale on the same surface.
  • Empty or partial matches explain which constraint blocked generation and suggest relaxations (wider equipment pool, shorter duration, etc.).

Acceptance checks

  • Task: “Build a 35-minute upper session with dumbbells only and justify each card” completable without external spreadsheets.
  • Observers can locate demo, mistake contrast, substitute, and intro for any card within two scroll lengths on mobile breakpoints.
  • Keyboard and screen-reader order follows visual order through generator → stack review → card detail.

FR2 · Real-time workout guidance (voice-first)

Objective: While training, the system delivers voice-led guidance—tempo, breathing prompts, inter-set timers, set/rep progress announcements, and completion feedback—so users can stay focused without constantly unlocking the phone. Visual UI remains a backup channel; the emphasis is on guiding, reminding, reporting, and acknowledging effort in real time. All coaching copy stays non-diagnostic and never implies clinical judgement.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • Voice cues fire on predictable beats (rep cadence, breath in/out, rest countdown, set transitions) with adjustable volume and speaking rate in the prototype.
  • Parallel on-screen text mirrors every critical voice cue for noisy gyms and d/Deaf users; captions stay within one tap during a set.
  • Pause, skip cue, and end-session controls remain reachable by touch and keyboard; voice cannot block emergency exits.
  • Optional mocked telemetry (e.g. heart-rate bands) may accompany voice but only with the same non-diagnostic disclaimer strings used elsewhere.

Acceptance checks

  • Blindfolded or phone-in-pocket walkthrough: trainee completes a scripted three-set block relying primarily on audio, confirming they heard tempo, breath, timer, and completion feedback.
  • With voice muted, users can still finish the block using captions alone within +20% time versus voiced run.
  • prefers-reduced-motion silences ornamental UI pulses but preserves timers, captions, and essential progress readouts.

FR3 · Lightweight gym connect

Objective: Around on-the-floor needs—quick help, knowledge sharing, and light social support—the product offers fast pathways for assistance, peer tips, low-friction interactions, and coach connection. The goal is faster access to answers, feedback, and a sense of presence while training, without turning the gym session into a heavy chat product.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • Surfaces distinguish help requests (e.g. “spot / form check”), knowledge snippets (short tips or links), and light reactions; each type has its own affordance so urgency is obvious.
  • Quick-help flows complete in ≤3 taps from the live workout context; optional coach routing respects availability states declared in the prototype.
  • Coach connection remains opt-in with explicit consent copy; revoking access clears pending requests and hides presence indicators immediately.
  • No mandatory always-on chat thread is required to finish a workout; asynchronous nudges expire after a configurable TTL.

Acceptance checks

  • Scenario test: user stalls on an unfamiliar machine and receives actionable peer or coach guidance without leaving the training timer surface.
  • Moderation-ready logging (mocked) records help vs social messages separately for coursework review.
  • Usability participants rate “felt supported on the floor” ≥ baseline questionnaire mean after using connect features in the demo script.

Playful Must-Haves

These playful must-haves pair with FR1–FR3: they specify how adaptive cards, voice guidance, and gym connect should feel—confident, human, and lightly delightful—without gamifying safety or medical nuance. Each item lists measurable acceptance checks for portfolio review and usability walkthroughs.

PH1 · Delight in “today’s stack”

Design intent (↔ FR1): Stacking adaptive cards should feel like curating a confident plan, not solving a spreadsheet. Micro-motion, typography, and celebratory copy reward good choices while keeping mistake contrasts and substitutes legible.

Expand acceptance criteria & mapped screens

Acceptance criteria:

  • When a stack is generated, a single “reveal” moment (animation ≤400ms or static fallback) previews the session story without hiding generator controls.
  • Card chrome uses colour + icon + text so stack states remain interpretable for colour-blind reviewers.
  • Copy never shames missed workouts; it reinforces autonomy (“Here’s what today unlocks”) rather than streak anxiety.

PH2 · Voice character & presence

Design intent (↔ FR2): The voice layer has a recognisable, calm persona—closer to a patient coach than a robotic timer. Micro-stingers (soft chimes, short affirmations) punctuate milestones without breaking concentration.

Expand acceptance criteria & mapped screens

Acceptance criteria:

  • Users can preview voice persona samples before starting a session; switching persona resets pending cues without desynchronising timers.
  • Affirmations stay under three seconds of speech and never overlap mandatory safety or breathing instructions.
  • With voice disabled, textual “persona” still uses the same supportive tone—no abrupt shift to clinical UI strings.

PH3 · Trusted floor companionship

Design intent (↔ FR3): Help, tips, and light social touches should feel like a supportive gym culture—not surveillance, spam, or performative flexing. Microcopy foregrounds consent, gratitude, and reciprocity.

Expand acceptance criteria & mapped screens

Acceptance criteria:

  • Help requests default to kind, direct language (“Need a quick spot?”) with opt-out reminders so users never feel nagged.
  • Positive reactions (emoji, canned cheers) send in ≤2 taps and never open a full-screen chat unless the user explicitly chooses “open thread.”
  • Coach connect surfaces show mutual availability and thank-you states so the relationship feels human, not transactional.

3. Ideation & Alternatives

Design Alternatives and Low-Fi Prototype

Alternative A: AR Pose Estimation

High novelty and potentially rich correction, but intrusive in a gym setting and dependent on camera framing, lighting, and privacy consent.

Alternative B: Adaptive Cards + Voice Guidance + Gym Connect

Chosen direction. It combines stackable adaptive workout planning, hands-free voice coaching, and lightweight on-floor connect so trainees get clarity, focus, and timely human support without a heavy social product.

Alternative C: Summary-Only Tracker

Useful for records, but too passive because it only explains performance after the moment when support was needed.

Design decision: We chose Alternative B because it best fits busy gym constraints. It reduces attention switching for coaches and gives trainees immediate, readable feedback without requiring cameras or complex wearable setup.

Crazy Eights

Crazy Eights is a structured divergence technique: each participant folds or divides paper into eight frames and sketches eight distinct ideas in a single timed round, about eight minutes total. We ran one facilitated round after we had aligned on trainee and coach pain points but before locking Alternative B, so sketches could stay deliberately rough and exploratory rather than “final UI.”

Our prompts focused TrainerSync-specific tensions—finding movements quickly on the gym floor, keeping encouragement visible during a heavy set, letting coaches scan risk without drowning in charts, and adding playful social signals that do not derail safety. After the silent sketch burst we clustered motifs (library entry patterns, live HUD layouts, coach radar summaries, lightweight reactions) and carried only the strongest patterns into the low-fi wireframe and HTML walkthrough.

How we verified the build against our requirements

  • Requirements traceability: Checked each themed requirement block (library + demos, live cues, map & coach oversight) plus the three playful must-haves against the high-fidelity gallery screens and corresponding routes on the hosted build.
  • Playful audit: Confirmed in-session surfaces on Live Training, lightweight reactions on Map/Companion, and recap affordances on Home/Profile/Coach list.
  • Walkthroughs: Two internal walkthroughs (trainee + coach) on the hosted build before usability testing; logged broken links and blocking flows.
  • Usability sessions: Three participant protocols (see Evaluation) exercised library→live→recap and coach monitoring paths; failures drove CSS hierarchy fixes.
  • Smoke checks: After AI-suggested CSS, resized viewport 320–1280px and tabbed through interactive controls on Chromium.

Ethical considerations for AI-generated code

Accessibility & clarity

Generated layouts often default to mouse-first interactions and colour-only status. We added dual-encoded labels for zones, preserved keyboard focus, and avoided presenting mock telemetry as clinical truth.

Bias & motivation framing

Models may suggest aggressive copy or stereotypical gym rhetoric. We replaced such drafts with neutral encouragement grounded in our questionnaire/interview notes, and kept coach visibility bounded by explicit binding/consent language.

5. Evaluation & Reflection

Usability Testing and Iterative Refinement

Three task-based usability sessions on the Alpha build: completion, errors, debrief notes, and SUS scores compared across participants (instrument background per Lewis, 2018; see References [10]).

Participant A: Beginner Trainee

Profile: undergraduate student, occasional gym user, mobile-first.

Task success: 4/5SUS: 76

Observed issue: The participant understood the workout library but hesitated before starting a live session because the CTA hierarchy was not strong enough.

Design implication: Make the session start action more prominent and use clearer intensity preview cues.

Participant B: Intermediate Trainee

Profile: regular gym user, familiar with workout apps, values progress tracking.

Task success: 5/5SUS: 82

Observed issue: The live screen was engaging, but the participant wanted a clearer explanation of what heart-rate colors meant.

Design implication: Add microcopy for target zones and make warnings more explainable.

Participant C: Coach Role Proxy

Profile: experienced gym user asked to complete coach monitoring tasks.

Task success: 4/5SUS: 74

Observed issue: The coach dashboard was useful, but priority ordering needed to be more obvious when several trainees required attention.

Design implication: Add clearer alert ranking, status labels, and one-click strategy dispatch.

Final Reflection

TrainerSync matters because it treats fitness technology as a shared human-centered service rather than a private tracking tool. The design can improve confidence, safety, and coach efficiency, but it also creates ethical responsibilities. Heart-rate cues and training status should not be framed as medical judgement. Users need consent controls, clear explanations of what data is visible to coaches, and boundaries around session history. The playful layer should support motivation without creating pressure, shame, or over-monitoring.

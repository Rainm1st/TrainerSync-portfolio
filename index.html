Project Overview

Project Architecture

TrainerSync is a shared web application: trainee discovery + live telemetry + coach oversight in one flow—smart filters and motion demos, session monitoring with timely cues, and a coach dashboard for bound trainees.

Why this architecture matters

The system is not only a workout library. It connects exercise discovery, live physiological feedback, location-based social interaction, and coach oversight into one continuous training flow.

Hosted system link

The high-fidelity web prototype is available at https://rainm1st.github.io/CPT208-TrainerSync/.

1. Motivation & Research

The Why

Authorship — non-substantive AI per CPT208: All motivation, gap analysis, research synthesis, requirements, ideation, evaluation, and reflections are original Team C3-6 work. Illustrations may use generative tools only as visuals and never replace our research logic. Vibe coding disclosure: Technical Implementation → System build: vibe coding. AI-assisted outputs were reviewed for fit with the brief and for safe, non-clinical wording around vitals and coaching.

Our group chose the Active Lifestyles track because gym training is a highly social, physically demanding, and feedback-sensitive activity, yet many existing digital tools only support the workout before it starts or after it ends. In real gym sessions, coaches often need to supervise multiple trainees at once, while trainees need quick reassurance that they are using the right equipment, moving at the right pace, and staying within a safe and effective training state. We saw a clear opportunity to design something more human-centered than a static fitness log or video library. TrainerSync was therefore conceived as a connected training experience that links the coach dashboard and trainee interface through real-time status cues, lightweight telemetry logic, and shared session feedback. We were also interested in the playful dimension of active systems: rather than only recording what happened, the system should shape motivation in the moment. This track fits our strengths because it allowed us to combine interface design, interaction flow, fitness-domain content, and service thinking into one coherent project. In short, we selected Active Lifestyles because it offers a meaningful design challenge where better interaction design can directly improve confidence, safety, coordination, and sustained engagement.

The Gap: Academic Papers

Source 3 Things Done Well 3 Things Missed
Angosto et al. (2023) Up-to-date synthesis of fitness-app adoption models (TAM/UTAUT variants); highlights hedonic motivation and gamification as satisfaction levers. Focus is intention-to-use surveys, not gym-floor coach workflows; limited detail on live supervision or spatial equipment finding.
Mazeas et al. (2022) Rigorous RCT meta-analysis on gamification for physical activity; reports durable (post-intervention) effects vs inactive and plain PA controls. Aggregates heterogeneous app designs; little guidance on coach dashboards, safety copy, or multi-user coordination.
Mair et al. (2023) Umbrella review linking digital health interventions to evidence-backed behaviour-change techniques (feedback, goals, social support). NCD framing dominates; gym coaching interfaces and playful social signals at point of exertion are under-specified.
Nishi et al. (2024) Large pooled evidence that digital apps—especially with gamification—can shift activity and cardiometabolic risk markers. Outcome-focused; does not prescribe UX for trainee–coach binding, live cues, or ethics around physiological displays.

Current Functional Requirements

Functional requirements = contractual “must do” behaviours. They outrank playful styling: the next subsection only shapes how those behaviours feel in use.

FR1 · Smart exercise filtering & motion demos

Objective: Trainees narrow the library by combining structured filters—for example selecting chest and cable station—so the list surfaces only matching movements. Each exercise ships with a still frame or looping GIF that shows the intended bar path and how force should feel, so correct execution is understandable at a glance before the working set.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • At least two independent filter dimensions (e.g. muscle group + equipment type) can be combined; results refresh in the same view without a full page reload in the HTML prototype.
  • Exercise detail pairs coaching cues with a still mock or looping GIF that illustrates trajectory and bracing—not generic stock imagery unrelated to the movement.
  • Empty states explain how to relax filters; the UI never fails silently when no exercises match the current combination.
  • Demo media stays inside the detail surface so navigation context (library → detail) is preserved.

Acceptance checks

  • Usability task: “Find a cable chest pattern and state the bar path you will use” completable without leaving TrainerSync.
  • Library chips and detail actions remain keyboard-operable with focus order matching visual order.
  • Touch targets for filter chips meet a ≥44px equivalent on the mobile breakpoint used in CPT208 demos.

FR2 · Live telemetry & in-session coaching cues

Objective: During a workout the app behaves like a digital monitoring panel: it tracks heart-rate trends (mocked in the prototype), current set/rep counts, and overall session progress. When the trainee enters a scripted exertion phase or crosses a configured heart-rate band, the UI surfaces short, concrete rhythm guidance—for example a breathing cue while locking out a heavy bench press—to help pacing and bracing. Copy remains motivational and non-diagnostic; it never implies clinical judgement.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • The live view animates or polls mock telemetry on a fixed cadence so reviewers can demonstrate “moving state” during walkthroughs.
  • Threshold or phase crossings trigger team-authored suggestions (breathing, tempo, brace reminders); wording avoids medical claims or personalised diagnoses.
  • Pause and end-session controls stay visible; coaching overlays must not hide emergency exits.
  • A short non-diagnostic disclaimer appears wherever numeric vitals are shown, aligned with Active Lifestyles ethics review.

Acceptance checks

  • Observers can distinguish green / amber / alert bands using text or iconography, not colour alone.
  • Test scripts require starting, pausing, and ending a session within two attempts on first exposure.
  • prefers-reduced-motion suppresses ornamental pulses while numeric readouts continue to update.

FR3 · Local map presence, quick reactions & coach remote oversight

Objective: With trainee-consented location enabled, the map highlights nearby coaches or gym partners so discovery stays local and opt-in. Trainees can exchange ultra-light reactions (emoji such as 🔥 or 💪) without opening a full chat thread. After an explicit bind, the coach handset can mirror the trainee’s live heart-rate and progress panel across distance for remote monitoring—complementing, not replacing, in-person supervision.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • Map and companion strips degrade gracefully when GPS is denied; mocked pins remain demo-ready for reviewers.
  • Emoji reactions send in ≤2 taps, stay optional, and never spawn a mandatory always-on chat.
  • Binding language states what the coach may view; revoking binding clears mirrored telemetry immediately in the prototype state machine.
  • The coach dashboard ranks bound trainees using priority signals (alerts, stale vitals, stalled sets) so attention ordering is testable.

Acceptance checks

  • Coach walkthrough: monitor three mocked trainees and justify who should be contacted first using only dashboard cues.
  • Trainee walkthrough: send a reaction mid-timer without blocking set completion controls.
  • Privacy copy lists every live field mirrored to the coach during an active bind, including heart-rate and progress widgets.

Playful Must-Haves

These three playful criteria are distinct from “features”: they describe how the experience should feel during and after movement—immediate, lightly social, and closing the loop on progress. Each item lists measurable acceptance checks we used when reviewing screens and usability tasks.

PH1 · In-session encouragement

Design intent: Motivation is sustained inside the workout, not postponed to a recap. Colour, motion, or microcopy should acknowledge effort without sounding clinical.

Expand acceptance criteria & mapped screens

Acceptance criteria:

  • During an active set or timed block, at least one encouragement surface stays visible without hiding primary controls such as pause or end.
  • Intensity or zone cues pair colour with text or icons so colour is not the only channel for accessibility.
  • Copy avoids medical claims; it describes rhythm, breathing cues, or session milestones in plain language.

PH2 · Light social signals

Design intent: Playfulness spreads through low-friction signals—emoji, cheers, or proximity context—that do not derail focus or expose unsafe oversharing.

Expand acceptance criteria & mapped screens

Acceptance criteria:

  • Users can send or receive a lightweight reaction in ≤2 taps from the map or companion strip.
  • Social interactions default to opt-in visibility; coach binding remains explicit.
  • No mandatory chat thread is required to finish a workout.

PH3 · Closed progress loop

Design intent: Every finished session produces an emotionally legible “what happened” moment—streaks, stats, or recap chips—so trainees feel closure and coaches get traceability.

Expand acceptance criteria & mapped screens

Acceptance criteria:

  • Ending a session reveals a summary card or dashboard delta within the same flow, without dead-ends.
  • At least one playful metric (streak, badge strip, or compact chart) is visible on Home or Profile.
  • Coach views surface enough history to praise or intervene without opening buried menus.

3. Ideation & Alternatives

Design Alternatives and Low-Fi Prototype

Alternative A: AR Pose Estimation

High novelty and potentially rich correction, but intrusive in a gym setting and dependent on camera framing, lighting, and privacy consent.

Alternative B: Telemetry-Driven Dashboard

Chosen direction. It supports low-friction coach visibility, clear trainee feedback, and fast intervention during a real session.

Alternative C: Summary-Only Tracker

Useful for records, but too passive because it only explains performance after the moment when support was needed.

Design decision: We chose Alternative B because it best fits busy gym constraints. It reduces attention switching for coaches and gives trainees immediate, readable feedback without requiring cameras or complex wearable setup.

Crazy Eights

Crazy Eights is a structured divergence technique: each participant folds or divides paper into eight frames and sketches eight distinct ideas in a single timed round, about eight minutes total. We ran one facilitated round after we had aligned on trainee and coach pain points but before locking Alternative B, so sketches could stay deliberately rough and exploratory rather than “final UI.”

Our prompts focused TrainerSync-specific tensions—finding movements quickly on the gym floor, keeping encouragement visible during a heavy set, letting coaches scan risk without drowning in charts, and adding playful social signals that do not derail safety. After the silent sketch burst we clustered motifs (library entry patterns, live HUD layouts, coach radar summaries, lightweight reactions) and carried only the strongest patterns into the low-fi wireframe and HTML walkthrough.

You are helping with a CPT208 static fitness web prototype called TrainerSync hosted on GitHub Pages. Stack: plain HTML/CSS/JS or minimal tooling only. Do NOT invent new product requirements.

Prompt A — shell & routing:
“Here is our screen list: Home, Exercise Library, Exercise Detail, Live Training, Map, Companion, Profile, Coach Dashboard, Trainee List, Live Detail, Messages. Create a shared nav/header pattern and separate HTML pages OR hash routes so each URL works when refreshed on GitHub Pages. Use relative paths only.”
Prompt B — Live Training panel:
“Implement the Live Training page from this wireframe snippet [paste]. Use mock data for heart rate (updates every 2s within 110–165 bpm). Show set counter and a non-medical disclaimer line we provide verbatim: ‘For wellness motivation only—not medical advice.’ Colours must match tokens #c8f542 accent and dark navy background.”
Prompt C — coach dashboard grid:
“Coach Dashboard needs responsive CSS grid: 2 columns on desktop, 1 column <768px. Cards show trainee name, status pill, sparkline placeholder. Fix overflow-x without horizontal scroll on mobile.”
Prompt D — map / companion stubs:
“Map page: embed a lightweight mock map container (static image or CSS grid) with clickable pins that open a small emoji reaction tray (fire, muscle, thumbs-up). No real geolocation API required—use fake coordinates.”
Prompt E — accessibility pass:
“Audit focus order for the Live Training controls (pause, end session). Add visible :focus outlines matching brand lime; ensure buttons have aria-labels where icons are textless.”
Prompt F — deployment bug:
“GitHub Pages returns 404 for nested routes /coach/live. Refactor to hash routing (#/coach/live) OR relative paths; show exact diff.”
Prompt G — playful UI polish:
“Add subtle CSS pulse on the heart-rate ring when crossing mock ‘amber’ threshold; keep reduced-motion fallback via prefers-reduced-motion.”

How we verified the build against our requirements

  • Requirements traceability: Checked each themed requirement block (library + demos, live cues, map & coach oversight) plus the three playful must-haves against the high-fidelity gallery screens and corresponding routes on the hosted build.
  • Playful audit: Confirmed in-session surfaces on Live Training, lightweight reactions on Map/Companion, and recap affordances on Home/Profile/Coach list.
  • Walkthroughs: Two internal walkthroughs (trainee + coach) on the hosted build before usability testing; logged broken links and blocking flows.
  • Usability sessions: Three participant protocols (see Evaluation) exercised library→live→recap and coach monitoring paths; failures drove CSS hierarchy fixes.
  • Smoke checks: After AI-suggested CSS, resized viewport 320–1280px and tabbed through interactive controls on Chromium.

Ethical considerations for AI-generated code

Accessibility & clarity

Generated layouts often default to mouse-first interactions and colour-only status. We added dual-encoded labels for zones, preserved keyboard focus, and avoided presenting mock telemetry as clinical truth.

Bias & motivation framing

Models may suggest aggressive copy or stereotypical gym rhetoric. We replaced such drafts with neutral encouragement grounded in our questionnaire/interview notes, and kept coach visibility bounded by explicit binding/consent language.

5. Evaluation & Reflection

Usability Testing and Iterative Refinement

Three task-based usability sessions on the Alpha build: completion, errors, debrief notes, and SUS scores compared across participants (instrument background per Lewis, 2018; see References [10]).

Participant A: Beginner Trainee

Profile: undergraduate student, occasional gym user, mobile-first.

Task success: 4/5SUS: 76

Observed issue: The participant understood the workout library but hesitated before starting a live session because the CTA hierarchy was not strong enough.

Design implication: Make the session start action more prominent and use clearer intensity preview cues.

Participant B: Intermediate Trainee

Profile: regular gym user, familiar with workout apps, values progress tracking.

Task success: 5/5SUS: 82

Observed issue: The live screen was engaging, but the participant wanted a clearer explanation of what heart-rate colors meant.

Design implication: Add microcopy for target zones and make warnings more explainable.

Participant C: Coach Role Proxy

Profile: experienced gym user asked to complete coach monitoring tasks.

Task success: 4/5SUS: 74

Observed issue: The coach dashboard was useful, but priority ordering needed to be more obvious when several trainees required attention.

Design implication: Add clearer alert ranking, status labels, and one-click strategy dispatch.

Final Reflection

TrainerSync matters because it treats fitness technology as a shared human-centered service rather than a private tracking tool. The design can improve confidence, safety, and coach efficiency, but it also creates ethical responsibilities. Heart-rate cues and training status should not be framed as medical judgement. Users need consent controls, clear explanations of what data is visible to coaches, and boundaries around session history. The playful layer should support motivation without creating pressure, shame, or over-monitoring.

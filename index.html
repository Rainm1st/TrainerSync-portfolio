Project Overview

System Architecture

TrainerSync guides a gym trainee through three phases in one mobile app: before training you Plan — browse and queue exercises by muscle group; during training you Monitor — track live heart rate with pacing cues and log completed sets; between equipment you Connect — check a live gym floor map and message peers to ask about availability or pre-book a turn.

Why this architecture matters

Plan workouts, Monitor live sessions, and Connect with gym peers — all in one mobile flow backed by a single Supabase real-time layer.

1. Motivation & Research

The Why

Authorship (CPT208): motivation through evaluation is Team C3-6. Research interpretation and design rationale are team-authored. Build: vibe coding. We checked fit to the brief and non-clinical wording on vitals and pacing cues.

Our group selected the Active Lifestyles track because we observed a persistent gap between workout planning and workout execution in real gym contexts. Many existing tools either support pre-session planning or post-session logging, but they provide limited support for live decision-making when users are already exercising. Through early interviews and pilot observations, we found that beginners often struggle with three practical problems: selecting exercises that match a target muscle group, maintaining training rhythm under fatigue, and coordinating equipment use without awkward social interruption. These problems are not only usability issues; they directly affect confidence, continuity, and adherence to healthy routines.

TrainerSync addresses this gap by treating the workout as a continuous service rather than a sequence of disconnected screens. The system combines exercise filtering and set logging, real-time in-session guidance with physiological monitoring, intelligent post-session reporting, and occupancy-aware social coordination in one mobile flow. We chose this direction because it allows us to test interaction design decisions that are central to human-centric computing: context-sensitive guidance, low-friction communication, and evidence-based iteration from observed behaviour. The Active Lifestyles track therefore fits both our domain interest and the course objective, enabling us to demonstrate how research evidence can be translated into tangible, evaluable interface decisions.

Advantages over current products

Advantage 1: End-to-end continuity

TrainerSync links exercise filtering, multi-select set logging, live movement guidance, and post-session reporting in one mobile workflow, instead of splitting planning and reflection into separate tools.

Advantage 2: Monitoring and recap linkage

The system combines in-session heart-rate monitoring with pace cues and a post-session intelligent summary, helping users interpret both current intensity and session-level outcomes.

Advantage 3: Equipment coordination

Occupancy visibility is combined with lightweight messaging to ask completed set counts and request next-turn pre-booking, which helps reduce waiting uncertainty and turn-taking friction.

Advantage 4: Low social friction

Friend/stranger messaging uses constrained intent types, making gym-floor communication easier to accept and less disruptive than ad-hoc face-to-face interruption.

User pain points that drove FR1 to FR3

Before defining features, we translated interview and alpha-testing evidence into three recurring pain clusters. Each functional requirement below is a direct response to one cluster.

Pain Point A: Broken training continuity

  • Novice trainees were often uncertain which exercises were appropriate for a specific target muscle group.
  • Beginners struggled to decide what to do next after filtering exercises.
  • Set completion was often remembered mentally, causing inaccurate progress tracking.
  • Post-workout reflection lacked a clear summary of what was actually completed.

Requirement response: FR1 introduces filter-to-plan flow, in-session action reordering, and per-movement set logging with real-time guidance cues.

Pain Point B: Guidance breaks during exertion

  • Users needed immediate pacing/breath cues while moving, not only before or after sessions.
  • Audio conditions were unstable in real gym environments (noise, headphones, interruptions).
  • When one channel failed, users lost confidence and task rhythm.

Requirement response: FR2 provides heart-rate monitoring with pace adjustment cues and intelligent end-of-session reporting.

Pain Point C: Equipment uncertainty and social coordination friction

  • Users could not reliably tell whether target equipment was occupied or nearly free.
  • Many users felt socially uncomfortable initiating face-to-face conversations with unfamiliar gym users during active workouts.
  • Constrained in-app messaging was more acceptable, as predefined intent types reduced social pressure and minimised unintended disruption to others.
  • Turn-taking was inefficient because set-count checks and pre-book requests were informal.

Requirement response: FR3 combines occupancy visibility with friend/stranger messaging for set checks and pre-booking.

Functional Requirements

FRs = must-do behaviour. PHs below = tone only.

FR1 · Plan — Exercise Filtering, Live Guidance & Set Logging

Objective: Pre: users filter and multi-select exercises to build a session queue. During: each selected movement shows live visual guidance, supports action reordering, and allows per-exercise set logging.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • Pre-session: Users filter by muscle group and equipment, then multi-select movements into a visible queue that can be reordered before start.
  • In-session: The current movement provides a live visual demo (image/GIF/video-style panel), while users record completed sets for each selected movement.
  • Continuity: Queue state shows done/current/upcoming items and preserves per-movement set records throughout the session.
  • In-session control: Users can reorder planned movements based on fatigue or equipment availability without losing recorded set history.
  • Empty or blocked generator states explain which constraint failed and propose relaxations.

Acceptance checks

  • Walkthrough: filter exercises → multi-select queue → start session → reorder actions → record sets.
  • Observers can locate movement demo, set-entry controls, and action-order controls within two scroll lengths on mobile breakpoints.
  • Keyboard and screen-reader order follows generator → queue → live execution → recap.

Feature evidence screenshots

FR2 · Monitor — Heart-Rate Monitoring & Session Summary

Objective: During session: monitor heart rate continuously and adjust pacing using breathing-light intensity cues. After session: generate an intelligent summary including recorded data and analysis highlights.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • In-session monitor: Heart-rate state remains visible and updates during active training blocks.
  • Pacing guidance: Breathing-light and intensity cues help users modulate pace under high exertion.
  • Session closure: Ending the session triggers a generated summary containing key workout data records.
  • Intelligent output: Summary text includes concise interpretation of workout performance, not only raw logs.

Acceptance checks

  • Walkthrough: run a scripted block while observing HR changes and pace cues across multiple set intervals.
  • On session end, the generated summary includes set logs, key intensity values, and readable analysis statements.
  • prefers-reduced-motion keeps essential readouts and status cues while reducing ornamental animation.

Feature evidence screenshots

FR3 · Connect — Equipment Occupancy & Peer Messaging

Objective: Users can check equipment occupancy in real time, then message other users (friends or strangers) to ask completed set counts and request advance booking/turn coordination.

Expand behaviours & acceptance checks

Behaviours & data rules

  • Map pins/zones display equipment category and occupancy ratio so users can quickly identify available or congested stations.
  • Users can open profile cards for both friends and strangers, with clear consent and visibility controls for contact initiation.
  • Messaging supports practical gym-floor coordination, including "how many sets done?" and "can I pre-book next turn?" queries.
  • Conversation actions are lightweight and task-oriented to reduce disruption during active workout periods.
  • Occupancy and messaging states remain linked so users can make immediate decisions about waiting, switching, or booking.

Acceptance checks

  • Scenario: user sees equipment marked busy, sends a message to current user, receives set-count reply, and decides whether to wait or switch.
  • Scenario: user sends a pre-booking request for next turn and receives confirmation/rejection without leaving the main map flow.
  • Friend and stranger messaging paths are both testable with clear consent state and message-status feedback.

5. Evaluation & Reflection

Usability Testing and Iterative Refinement

Three Alpha tasks—success, errors, debrief, SUS (Lewis 2018 · Ref [8]).

This section reports what was tested (task flow), how performance was measured (success + SUS), which issues were observed, and what UI refinements were applied after each finding.

Usability Testing

Participant A: Beginner Trainee

Profile: undergraduate student, occasional gym user, mobile-first.

Task success: 4/5SUS: 76

Task focus: complete a full trainee flow from exercise selection to live-session start and basic in-session control.

Issue observed: the participant hesitated before entering Live Training because the start control had low visual salience and intensity expectations were not explicit.

Refinement implemented: we strengthened the primary CTA hierarchy, added an intensity preview line before session entry, and simplified adjacent secondary actions to reduce decision friction.

Post-change effect: in re-check, route finding was faster and the participant reported higher confidence about "what happens next" before pressing start.

Participant B: Intermediate Trainee

Profile: regular gym user, familiar with workout apps, values progress tracking.

Task success: 5/5SUS: 82

Task focus: interpret live heart-rate feedback, adjust pacing, and finish a session while reading recap signals.

Issue observed: the participant completed tasks successfully but reported ambiguity in colour semantics (especially amber vs red), reducing trust in quick interpretation under exertion.

Refinement implemented: we added explicit zone microcopy, clarified threshold wording, and improved warning label contrast so colour is supported by text rather than used as the only signal.

Post-change effect: interpretation errors decreased in follow-up walkthroughs, and the participant could explain zone status without relying on trial-and-error.

Participant C: Intermediate Trainee

Profile: regular gym user asked to complete occupancy and social-coordination tasks.

Task success: 4/5SUS: 74

Task focus: check occupancy, message nearby users, and coordinate set-check / pre-book actions with minimal interruption.

Issue observed: the participant could not consistently identify the next coordination action because occupancy and message-status cues were too visually similar.

Refinement implemented: we clarified occupancy-state chips, separated message-intent actions, and shortened the quick-action path for set-check and pre-book requests.

Post-change effect: coordination choices became more consistent, and response steps required fewer interactions in repeated task runs.

Final Reflection

TrainerSync succeeded in demonstrating that the three pain points identified in research — broken training continuity, unreliable in-session guidance, and equipment coordination friction — can be addressed within a single coherent mobile workflow. The Plan → Monitor → Connect flow proved evaluable end-to-end: participants could complete meaningful tasks in each feature, and observed issues were traceable back to specific design decisions rather than arbitrary implementation choices. The Supabase real-time presence layer in particular worked reliably across all test sessions, enabling the gym floor map to reflect live occupancy without requiring manual refresh.

The most significant limitation was the use of simulated heart-rate data. Because the prototype generates mock HR values rather than reading from a wearable, the Monitor feature could only be evaluated for interaction design quality — not for physiological accuracy or real-time responsiveness under actual exertion. Similarly, the gym floor map relies on a static SVG overlay and manually positioned presence data rather than real GPS or Bluetooth indoor positioning, which means occupancy counts in the Connect feature are representative but not live in a true deployment sense.

If we were to continue development, the highest-value next step would be integrating a BLE heart-rate sensor via the Web Bluetooth API to replace mock HR with real data. This would make the breathing-light pacing cue and zone-display genuinely responsive to the trainee's physiological state rather than a visual simulation. A secondary priority would be replacing the static floor plan with a real indoor positioning layer — either BLE beacon triangulation or Wi-Fi RSSI — so that the gym map reflects actual equipment zone density in real time.

On the design process side, the iteration from alpha to current high-fidelity confirmed that vibe-coded scaffolding is productive for early UI exploration, but requires deliberate human review at each stage to catch misalignments between generated code and stated requirements. In our case, the most important review moments were wording checks on HR-related copy (to avoid clinical framing), contrast and accessibility passes on zone colour coding, and consent-flow verification for the friend/stranger messaging path. These were team-authored decisions that the AI tools could not make independently, and they represent the core of what makes the interaction design human-centric rather than merely functional.
